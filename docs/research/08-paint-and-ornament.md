# 08 — Paint Scheme, Decoration and Materials (PBR authoring reference)

**Subject:** HMS SURPRISE, ex-French corvette UNITÉ (launched Le Havre 16 Jan 1794, designer Pierre‑Alexandre Forfait, builder Jean Fouache; captured by HMS *Inconstant* 20 Apr 1796; commissioned RN June 1796; sold at Deptford February 1802). Source: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Surprise_(1796)>

**Target look:** the Model Shipyard (Stephens & Kenau) museum model — black topsides, gunports set *into* the black, thin ochre sheer mouldings, copper below the waterline, gilt at head/stern, white boats, blue ensign.

**Unit rule used throughout:** 1 ft = 0.3048 m, 1 in = 0.0254 m.

**Confidence key:** `[S]` = sourced fact. `[D]` = derived/computed from a sourced fact. `[R]` = **RECONSTRUCTED** — not documented for this ship; derived from a stated period rule-of-thumb. Never present `[R]` values as fact.

## 1. ROYAL NAVY PAINTING PRACTICE 1775–1815

### 1.1 Timeline of orders and practice

| Date | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1715 | Admiralty order attempting to standardise ship colours — "largely ignored" | <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Chequer> |
| 27 May 1780, modified 13 Jul 1780 | Navy Board orders to Dockyard Officers on paint. A further 1780 Admiralty order **allowed captains to paint in yellow *or* black** | cited via <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00253359.2020.1712841> (Vale, *Mariner's Mirror* 106:1) and <https://www.warlordgames.com/painting-vessels-from-the-age-of-sail/> |
| 1780s–1790s | Captains' latitude: unrated vessels and most frigates could be "all white, all yellow ochre, all red, all black, or all dark blue‑grey", but **more commonly black hulls with a coloured stripe** in yellow, red, pale orange, white, green or light blue | <https://www.warlordgames.com/painting-vessels-from-the-age-of-sail/> |
| 1796 | **Admiralty attempted to abolish figureheads on new ships**, substituting scroll/billet heads; "the order was not wholly complied with" | <https://heritagecalling.com/2019/01/31/a-brief-history-of-ships-figureheads/> |
| c.1790s–1805 | "Nelson chequer": black gunport lids painted onto the yellow strake, so the side reads **striped when ports shut, chequered when open** | <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Chequer> |
| 1805 | Nelson asked for **more white in the ochre to make it paler**; he died before the Admiralty ruled, and the request was rejected | Andrew Baines (NMRN), <https://www.historyanswers.co.uk/history-of-war/hms-victory-returns-to-her-1805-colours-and-nelson-hated-them/> |
| 1816 | Victory's ochre/black replaced by **black and white** — the post‑Georgian norm | same |

**Overall arc** (Vale, *Mariner's Mirror* 106:1, 2020, abstract): *"the colour scheme of British warships between 1775 and 1815 changed from yellow hulls, through the yellow and black Nelson chequer to the ubiquitous black and white; while the interiors and bulwarks were first painted red, then yellow ochre, then a range of light colours including green."* <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00253359.2020.1712841>

Companion scholarly source (paywalled, abstract only): Peter Goodwin, "The Application and Scheme of Paintworks in British Men‑of‑War in the Late Eighteenth and Early Nineteenth Centuries", *Mariner's Mirror* 99:3 (2013) <https://doi.org/10.1080/00253359.2013.815993> — uses paint‑sample analysis plus a **1805 carpenter's account book**. *Full text not obtainable; both Tandfonline and the SNR site return 403 to automated fetch. Flagged as an unread primary source.*

### 1.2 "Bright" (varnished) vs painted — and what "black" actually was

- **Wales were not painted black in the modern sense.** They were **"daubed with a mixture of lamp‑black and tar, which gave the effect of black paint."** Reported in discussion citing period practice: <https://modelshipworld.com/topic/2364-mast-colours/> (via search index; site 403s automated fetch). Corroborated: "Wales were tarred or otherwise blackened; as they had been from Greek and Roman times." <https://nwredhead.com/why-were-ships-painted-black-and-yellow/>
- **"Bright" work** = wood preserved rather than painted: *"Natural wood… was treated for preservation—'bright'—with a combination of linseed oil, pine tar, and tallow, or something similar, making it dark brown or dark red‑brown."* <https://timberandsail.com/wp-content/uploads/how-to-paint-historically-accurate-17th-18th-century-ships.pdf> p.2
- **Consequence for PBR:** the "black" of a 1796 topside is a **tar‑loaded, semi‑gloss, very dark warm brown-black**, not neutral #000 and not matte. It streaks and bleeds in the sun. Model it as base ~#1C1613, roughness 0.45–0.55, with vertical tar-run streaking in the roughness map.
- The reference model's **"black finish"** is a modern acrylic, flatter and cooler than the historical tar. If matching the model exactly, use ~#141414 @ roughness 0.60; if matching history, use the warm value above.

All hex values below are **sRGB, gamma-encoded**, i.e. what goes in a base-colour texture. Historic paints were hand‑ground in linseed oil; batch variation was large. Treat these as centres of a range, not as absolutes.

### 2.2 Confirmation: were the inboard works and port-lid interiors red? — YES

Three independent statements:

1. *"Until the Revolutionary War with France (1793–1801), the inside of the port‑lid in the British Navy was red, as also were the sides of the ship. The reason… any blood spilled in action would not show against the red paint."* <https://www.oxfordreference.com/viewbydoi/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803095912579> `[S]`
2. *"Interiors and bulwarks were first painted red, then yellow ochre, then a range of light colours including green."* Vale abstract <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00253359.2020.1712841> `[S]`
3. Modelling standard: *"for all gunports, the linings are to be painted red ochre, and for gunports with lids, the linings are painted red ochre and the… lip should be the colour of the surrounding ship's side."* <http://www.jotika-ltd.com/Manuals/Victory_Hull.pdf> `[S]`

**Consequence for a port-bow-under-sail render:** ports on the disengaged side are normally **shut** at sea. If any lid is triced up, the underside of the lid and the port lining flash **red ochre `#913832`** against the black side — this is the single strongest colour accent on the whole topside, and it is easy to get wrong. The lid's *outboard* face and its perimeter lip are black.

Note the transitional date: **1793–1801 is exactly Surprise's RN service window**, so red is correct for her, and ochre-inboard is correct only for the later fictional Surprise of the novels/film.

### 3.4 Laying pattern and nailing

- **"Arranged as bricks are arranged in a wall"** — running bond, courses horizontal-ish, staggered. <https://www.sobco.com/ship_model/articles/coppering.htm> `[S]`
- **Overlap direction:** the upper sheet's lower edge laps *over* the lower sheet's upper edge; vertical joints **face aft** so water flows over, not into, the lap — the "fish scale" effect. <https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2016/11/18/new-copper-sheathing-2/> `[S]`
- **Stagger is NOT a clean half-lap:** *"The overlap between the upper sheets is not generally centered above the lower sheets since the rows of sheets on the hull are of different lengths because of the curve of the hull."* <https://www.sobco.com/ship_model/articles/coppering.htm> `[S]` → **randomise the horizontal offset per course** (±20–40% of sheet length) rather than tiling a perfect brick pattern. A perfect brick pattern is the classic tell of a CG copper bottom.
- **Gores/goring belts:** wedge-shaped cut sheets fill the triangular convergence at bow and stern. <https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2016/11/18/new-copper-sheathing-2/> `[S]`
- **Nails:** copper (later brass, stronger) sheathing nails, **1⅛ in and 1¼ in (28.6 and 31.8 mm)** long; iron was tried first and rusted out. Search index of <https://www.history.navy.mil/…/copper-sheathing.html> `[S]`; <https://www.oldcopper.org/special_topics/hms_victory_copper_sheathing.php> `[S]`
- **Nail pattern:** a ring of holes around the sheet perimeter, **plus interior rows** for security. <https://www.sobco.com/ship_model/articles/coppering.htm> `[S]`
- **Nail count per sheet:** **NOT FOUND in any source.** `[R]` estimate: perimeter of a 14×48 in sheet = 124 in; at a period-plausible 1.5–2 in pitch that is **62–83 perimeter nails**, plus one or two interior longitudinal rows of ~24–32 each → **~90–140 nails per sheet**. Rule used: sheathing-nail pitch on surviving copper is of the order of the nail length ×1.5. **Mark as reconstructed.** Nail heads are ~0.25 in (6 mm) — render them as a normal/roughness detail only.
- **Consistency warning:** *"multiple crews working simultaneously to copper a ship means that things were not consistent"* and *"there is no 18th century plan of the layout of the copper sheathing."* <https://www.sobco.com/ship_model/articles/coppering.htm>, <https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2016/11/18/new-copper-sheathing-2/> `[S]` → licence to vary.

### 3.5 Extent up the hull

- Copper ran **from the keel up to the load waterline plus a margin** — the boot-top band, *"the painted band on a vessel just above and slightly below the waterline"* <https://thetidesofhistory.com/2023/05/10/sailor-speak-of-the-week-boot-top/> `[S]`
- Installation ran **simultaneously downward from the waterline and upward from the keel**, meeting mid-hull. <https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2016/11/18/new-copper-sheathing-2/> `[S]`
- **Exact height above LWL for Surprise: NOT FOUND.** `[R]` **2 ft 6 in – 3 ft 0 in (0.76 – 0.91 m)** above the load waterline at midships, sweeping up at bow and stern to follow the sheer and stay above the deep-laden and heeled waterlines. Rule: the sheathing line is drawn on RN draughts roughly parallel to and a little above the LWL, high enough that a heeled frigate never exposes bare plank; the sweep is greater forward than aft. Rule source: general practice described at <https://www.sobco.com/ship_model/articles/coppering.htm> and the boot-top definition above. **The exact line is on the RMG lines-and-profile plan J5948 (John Marshall, Plymouth) — obtain that plan to replace this reconstruction.** <https://prints.rmg.co.uk/products/plan-of-hms-surprise-1796-lines-and-profile-j5948>
- **Above the copper line and below the wales** there is a narrow band of exposed planking/black stuff — this is the boot-top. On the reference model it reads as a thin dark line separating copper from black.

### 3.6 COLOUR PROGRESSION — hex per stage

Atmospheric weathering sequence (roofing copper, well documented): *"from the natural salmon pink colour through a series of russet brown shades to light and dark chocolate browns and finally to a dark, dull slate grey or dull black"* <https://www.jeelix.com/color-guide-to-copper-brass-and-bronze/> / <https://momentum.providence.edu/all-completed-projects/copper-roofs/> `[S]`. Twelve-step timeline: salmon pink (new) → brown (4 mo) → rosette brown (8 mo) → deep rosette (1 yr) → chocolate (2 yr) → dark chocolate (3 yr) → rusty brown (4 yr) → green with brown (5 yr) → dark green (7 yr) → leaf green (10 yr) → blue-green (15 yr) → bright green (25–30 yr). <https://www.sequoia-brass-copper.com/copper-weathering-components/> `[S]`

| Stage | Description | Hex (base colour) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. New, as-fitted** | bright salmon-pink / pinkish-orange — copper's true colour when freshly cut | **`#F7BC9E`** | `[S]` — Filament PBR metal base colour for copper, linear (0.97, 0.74, 0.62) <https://google.github.io/filament/Materials.md.html> |
| 0b. New, alternative reference | same, from the classic Unreal/Filament F0 (0.955, 0.637, 0.538) | `#FAD1C2` | `[D]` — linear→sRGB conversion of the published F0. Slightly pinker; use if the render feels too orange. |
| 1. Days–weeks (cuprous oxide film) | still pink but dulled | `#D99C82` | `[R]` — 15% desaturation of stage 0. Rule: "a thin film of pinkish cuprous oxide (Cu₂O) forms quickly within hours to days" <https://www.jeelix.com/color-guide-to-copper-brass-and-bronze/> |
| **2. Months — russet / rosette brown. ← MATCHES THE REFERENCE PHOTO** | warm mid brown with copper still glinting | **`#A8603E`** | `[R]` — interpolated on the sourced salmon→chocolate ramp <https://www.sequoia-brass-copper.com/copper-weathering-components/> |
| 3. 2–3 yr chocolate brown | dark, low-chroma | `#6B3E2A` | `[R]` — same ramp |
| 4. 3–4 yr dark chocolate / cupric oxide (CuO) | near-black brown | `#3E2418` | `[R]` — "continued oxidation produces black cupric oxide (CuO), deepening the colour into a russet or dark chocolate brown" <https://www.jeelix.com/color-guide-to-copper-brass-and-bronze/> |
| 5. 5–7 yr green-with-brown → dark green | patina appearing | `#5E6B49` | `[R]` — same ramp |
| 6. 10–15 yr verdigris | the classic green | `#43B3AE` | `[S]` <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdigris> |
| 6b. Verdigris, painterly/greener variant | | `#9DB3A1` | `[S]` Behr "Copper Patina" <https://encycolorpedia.com/9db3a1> |

**CRITICAL CAVEAT — immersed copper does not weather like a roof.** The twelve-step table above is atmospheric. A ship's bottom is **continuously eroding by design**: *"the green oxide formed on copper sheathing is a benefit… the oxide prevents the adhesion of barnacles because it is very poisonous"* <https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/ship/copper-sheathing.htm> `[S]`. The sacrificial surface constantly sloughs, so an in-service bottom sits at **stage 2–4 (russet to dark chocolate)** and rarely reaches a stable green. Green verdigris on a ship's bottom is a **model-maker's convention**, not a sea-going reality; where green does appear it is on the **boot-top band at and just above the waterline**, which is alternately wetted and dried.

**Recommendation for this render:** the reference photo reads natural / lightly weathered. Use **`#A8603E` (stage 2)** as the base colour with `#F7BC9E` bleeding through on the high-curvature bow and turn of bilge where the plates are polished by water flow, and `#3E2418` in the laps and low areas. Zero green.

**PBR settings for copper:** metalness **1.0** (Filament: *"this property should be used as a binary value, set to either 0 or 1"* <https://google.github.io/filament/Materials.md.html>). Roughness **0.35** for new/polished, **0.55–0.70** for the weathered oxide, varying per sheet — the per-sheet roughness variation is what sells hand-laid copper.

## 4. FIGUREHEAD

### 4.1 What we know — and do not know

**The figurehead of the 1796 Surprise / 1794 Unité is NOT DOCUMENTED in any source reachable here.** This is a genuine gap, not a search failure. Wikipedia's article on the ship *"contains no information about the ship's figurehead or decorative elements"* <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Surprise_(1796)>. The RMG plan records (J5948 lines-and-profile, and the deck/quarter/forecastle plan, both by John Marshall, Master Shipwright Plymouth 1795–1801) carry no descriptive text about carving in their online catalogue entries <https://prints.rmg.co.uk/products/plan-of-hms-surprise-1796-lines-and-profile-j5948>. A sheer draught *may* show the head profile — **acquiring plan J5948 is the single highest-value next step for this topic.**

### 4.2 The "female figure with sword and shield" — DO NOT ATTRIBUTE TO THE HISTORICAL SHIP

Search results repeatedly surface *"a female figure wielding a sword in her left hand and a shield in her right."* Tracing it: it belongs to the **replica** — the 1970 vessel built as **HMS *Rose***, re-carved and re-dressed as *Surprise* for the 2003 film, now at the Maritime Museum of San Diego. *"During modifications for the film, the figurehead was replaced."* <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Surprise_(replica_ship)>, <https://sdmaritime.org/visit/the-ships/hms-surprise/>, photo <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:HMS_Surprise_(replica_ship)_figurehead_2009-12_1.JPG>. It is a **1970s/2002 film-production design**, not a 1794 artefact. Wikipedia's own article on the 1796 ship notes only that *"a replica of HMS Rose was painted in 1805 colours to represent Surprise"* — 1805 colours, i.e. the chequer, which the real ship never wore.

### 4.3 What Marquardt drew

Karl Heinz Marquardt produced **"more than 50 specially commissioned line plans"** for Brian Lavery & Geoff Hunt, *The Frigate Surprise: The Design, Construction and Careers of Jack Aubrey's Favourite Command* (Conway/Norton, 2008; ISBN 9781844860746) <https://wwnorton.com/books/9780393070620>, <https://www.abebooks.co.uk/9781844860746/Frigate-Surprise-Brian-Lavery-1844860744/plp>. Geoff Hunt's paintings are *"based on research of the original plans of the Surprise as sketched during the ship's refitting at Plymouth in 1798"* <https://soundingsonline.com/features/the-frigate-surprise/>. **Whether Marquardt's sheets include a head/figurehead elevation could not be confirmed from any online preview** — Google Books offers no accessible snippet and the model forums that quote the book return 403 to automated fetch. **This book is the definitive source and should be bought.** Third-party model kits state that they *"reproduce the ornate stern and the figurehead"* from that research (Artesanía Latina 1:48, product 62576).

### 4.4 What she plausibly carried — RECONSTRUCTED

`[R]` **French corvette *Unité*, 1794.** *Unité* = "Unity", one of the Revolutionary abstractions (Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité, Unité). Revolutionary-era French naval carving replaced royal and mythological figures with **allegorical classical female figures** and Phrygian-cap republican emblems. A plausible original: a **standing or half-length classical female in flowing drapery**, possibly with a *faisceau* (fasces — the Revolutionary emblem of unity: rods bound together) or clasped hands. Rule: French ships were named for the concept and the head carved to personify it; and the state-property mark of Revolutionary France was applied literally (cf. the French government canvas of 1736 with dark selvedge threads identifying state property, <https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2016/07/19/canvasing-the-navy/>). **This is inference from the ship's name and period iconography, not evidence.**

`[R]` **What the RN would have done in 1796.** Two possibilities, and the timing is awkward:

1. **Kept the French head**, repainted. Captured ships were commonly taken into service with their existing head; recarving was an expense the Navy Board avoided. This is the **most likely** outcome for a prize commissioned within two months of capture (captured 20 Apr, commissioned June 1796).
2. **Replaced with a scroll/billet head.** *"In 1796, the Admiralty tried to abolish figureheads altogether on new ships, but the order was not wholly complied with."* <https://heritagecalling.com/2019/01/31/a-brief-history-of-ships-figureheads/> The order targeted **new ships**, so a prize is arguably outside it — but 1796 is exactly the year, and a 24-gun post ship is exactly the class where economy bit hardest. *"By around 1700… the Royal Navy began the process of restricting ornamental carving around the bow and stern"* and *"decoration on lower-ranking warships was curtailed"* <https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/ship-figureheads-decoration>.
3. If recarved as *Surprise*: the RN convention for an abstract-noun name was a **classical female personification**. A "Surprise" figure would plausibly be a female with a startled gesture — raised hand, turned head.

**Modelling recommendation:** build a **modest classical female half-figure with trailboard scrollwork**, sized small — *"the scale was drastically reduced and… naval figureheads became increasingly isolated pieces of ornament with very limited trailboard decoration"* <https://heritagecalling.com/2019/01/31/a-brief-history-of-ships-figureheads/>. Material: **pine** — *"the Navy Board stipulated that pine be used in lieu of these heavier materials and, eventually, figureheads were almost exclusively made of pine"* <https://www.history.navy.mil/content/history/museums/nmusn/explore/artifacts/artifact-curios/sculptures/figureheads.html>; elm/oak earlier, then yellow pine <https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/ship-figureheads-decoration>. **Document it in the model's notes as a reconstruction.**

### 4.5 Finish of the figurehead and carved work

- Period reality: *"Gilding was exceptionally rare. 'Gold' carvings and rails were instead painted 'gold colour'—**yellow varnish over white**—or **yellow ochre**."* <https://timberandsail.com/wp-content/uploads/how-to-paint-historically-accurate-17th-18th-century-ships.pdf> p.2 `[S]`
- The reference model uses **cold-cast bronze ornaments** <https://www.stephensandkenau.com/ship/hms-surprise/> `[S]` — a modeller's convention reading as real metal.
- **Two valid targets, pick one and be consistent:**
  - **Historically honest:** ochre-yellow carving, `#BB9476` → `#CC7722`, **metalness 0.0**, roughness 0.35 (varnished), with a warm clear-coat. Reads gold-ish under sun without being metal.
  - **Match the reference model:** true gilt, **metalness 1.0**, base `#D4AF37` or Filament gold `#FFD891`, roughness 0.25–0.40. Add dark ochre in the recesses so the relief reads.
- Figurehead **flesh** on RN heads of the period was commonly painted white/off-white lead when the whole head was not gilt: `#E4E8DC`. `[R]` (from the general availability and cheapness of white lead; not specifically attested for this ship).

### 5.3 Stern lanterns

- **NOT FOUND for Surprise, and probably NOT CARRIED.** `[R]` Poop/stern lanterns were a **flag-officer and ship-of-the-line** fitting: three on a flagship's taffrail, one on a ship of the line. A 24-gun sixth-rate post ship without a poop deck would normally carry **none** as permanent taffrail ornament; night station-keeping was done with a temporary hoisted lantern. Rule source: no reachable source specifies lantern fits by rate; the *Anatomy of the Ship* volumes (David White, *The Frigate Diana*; Brian Lavery, *The 74-Gun Ship Bellona*) are the documented place to check and were not obtainable here. **Flagged as unresolved.**
- If the reference model shows a lantern, treat it as a modeller's addition. Glazing would be **horn or glass in a copper/tinned frame**; render frame as metal, roughness 0.5, base `#8A6B4A`.

### 6.2 Blue Ensign specifics

- **What it was:** *"Prior to 1864, the plain blue ensign had been the ensign of one of three squadrons of the Royal Navy, the Blue Squadron."* <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Ensign> `[S]` — squadron seniority red > white > blue, established by order of 14 January 1653 <https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/gb-ensh1.html> `[S]`
- **Whether Surprise specifically wore blue: NOT VERIFIED.** She wore whatever her admiral's squadron colour was, and that changed with station. **A blue ensign is entirely plausible and is what the reference model shows** — accept it, but do not state it as documented.
- **Field colour:** modern Flag Institute spec is Pantone 280 C = **`#012169`**, red Pantone 186 C = **`#C8102E`**, white unspecified <https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/uk-flags/union-flag-specification/>, <https://www.flagcolorcodes.com/united-kingdom> `[S]`
- **Period bunting was duller.** `[R]` Wool bunting, indigo/woad-dyed and madder-dyed, sun-bleached and salt-washed: blue **`#22375E`**, red **`#A32D34`**, white **`#E8E2D4`**. Rule: natural dyes on loose-woven wool bunting have far lower chroma and higher lightness-variance than modern Pantone-matched nylon, and a flag at sea fades within weeks. Use the Flag Institute values as the *maximum* saturation and desaturate 20–30%.

| Corner reinforcement | clew, tack, earing patches — multiple layers, visibly thicker and darker | — | `[R]` |

Sail-count sanity check from a documented ship: *Constitution*'s **foretopsail alone took 23 bolts (828 yd)** and the full suit **~350 bolts (12,600 yd)** <https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2016/07/19/canvasing-the-navy/> `[S]`. Scaled by sail area, a 24-gun post ship's suit is roughly **120–160 bolts** `[R]`.

### 7.4 Full vs furled — shading behaviour

- **Full / drawing:** the cloth is under tension, so seam lines pull **straight and taut**; the belly is a smooth curve broken only by the seams and the reef bands. Backlit from ahead the sail is strongly **translucent** — the seams, bolt rope, reef bands and any patches read as **dark bars through the cloth**. This is the single most important effect for a "port bow under full sail" render: author a **transmission/subsurface** value of ~0.3–0.4 on the sail material and put the seam/band geometry into the *opacity* channel as well as the normal.
- Wind pressure creates a **hard leech curl** and diagonal tension creases running from the clews toward the head.
- **Furled:** the sail is bunched along the yard in **irregular vertical rolls** with the bunt gathered heaviest amidships of the yard; the foot is rolled up and gasketed. Seam geometry is invisible; the read is entirely folds and shadow. Roughness rises (the cloth is compressed and shaded) and translucency effectively goes to zero.
- **Weathering:** dirt collects at the **foot and the corners**, salt-bloom whitens the leeches, and repair patches are a **different tone** from the surrounding field — patchwork tonal variation is what makes a period sail look real rather than printed.
